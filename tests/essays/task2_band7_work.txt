Remote work has become widespread since the pandemic, prompting debate about whether it should remain the default model. While working from home offers clear advantages for many employees, I disagree that it should completely replace the traditional office.

On the one hand, remote work increases flexibility and can improve wellbeing by cutting commuting time. Employees may concentrate better without office distractions, and organisations can recruit talent from a wider geography. For parents and people with disabilities, home working can also remove barriers that once limited participation.

On the other hand, offices still play a vital role in collaboration, mentoring, and organisational culture. Informal conversations often generate ideas that video calls rarely replicate. Junior staff in particular may struggle to develop professionally without face-to-face guidance. Moreover, some roles—laboratory work, manufacturing, and emergency services—cannot be performed remotely at all.

A balanced approach is therefore preferable: hybrid models that reserve office days for teamwork while allowing focused tasks to be done at home. In conclusion, remote work is a powerful option, but treating it as a universal default would ignore the social and practical strengths of shared workplaces.

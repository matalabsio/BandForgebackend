Whether technology has simplified or complicated contemporary life remains a contested issue. While some contend that digital tools have introduced unnecessary complexity and pressure, others argue that they have removed friction from everyday tasks. Both perspectives contain merit, yet the overall effect depends largely on how individuals and institutions manage these tools.

Critics emphasise the cognitive and social costs of constant connectivity. Notifications fragment attention, remote work can erode leisure, and social platforms often amplify comparison rather than genuine connection. In this sense, devices marketed as time-saving can lengthen the workday and weaken mental recovery.

Conversely, technology has clearly streamlined many routine activities. Online banking, digital healthcare booking, and ubiquitous information search reduce travel and waiting. Learners in remote regions can access high-quality instruction that was previously unavailable, and collaboration across borders is now routine for many professions.

In my opinion, technology is neither inherently simplifying nor inherently complicating; outcomes follow from habits and policy. Where boundaries are deliberate—notification limits, focused work blocks, and digital literacy—technology tends to reduce friction. Without such discipline, the same tools can make life feel noisier and more demanding. Societies should therefore invest as much in user education and workplace norms as in the devices themselves.

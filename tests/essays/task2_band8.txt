The question of whether university education should be free remains contentious. Advocates argue that removing tuition fees expands opportunity and strengthens the knowledge economy, whereas opponents warn of fiscal strain and diluted standards. On balance, I support publicly funded higher education with carefully designed accountability measures.

Free tuition can reduce socioeconomic barriers that currently prevent capable students from entering competitive programmes. When education is treated as a public investment rather than a private purchase, societies benefit from a more skilled workforce and higher long-term tax revenue. Countries with low or no fees have demonstrated that broad access need not preclude research excellence.

Nevertheless, unrestricted free provision can create inefficiencies. Rising enrolment may overwhelm teaching capacity, and students who face no financial stake might choose courses casually. A pragmatic model would combine free tuition with means-tested living support and performance thresholds, ensuring resources reach motivated learners while protecting institutional quality.

Ultimately, the benefits of an educated citizenry outweigh the short-term budget pressures of fee abolition. With transparent funding and academic safeguards, free university education can be both equitable and sustainable.

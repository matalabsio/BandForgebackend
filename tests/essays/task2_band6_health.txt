Many countries are facing rising rates of obesity, and people disagree about whether governments should tax junk food. While some argue that personal choice must be respected, I believe a carefully designed tax can improve public health when combined with education.

Supporters of a junk-food tax claim it would raise the price of sugary drinks and fatty snacks, encouraging consumers to buy healthier options. Revenue could then fund school meal programmes or sports facilities. Evidence from sugar taxes in several countries suggests modest reductions in soft-drink consumption.

Critics respond that such taxes unfairly hit low-income families, who already spend a larger share of income on food. They also note that people may simply switch to other unhealthy products. In this view, education and clearer labelling are fairer tools.

In my opinion, a tax alone is not enough, but it can complement education and wider access to affordable fresh food. Governments should therefore use modest taxes as one part of a broader strategy rather than a single solution.
